OpenClassrooms · Companion

Why learners weren't talking to their AI companion

  • AI Product Design
  • Mixed-method Research
  • Summer 2024
  • Shipped

Overview

Companion is OpenClassrooms' AI assistant, built into every project so learners can get help with the context of their own work. It launched on all paths in July 2024. A week later, usage was already falling. I led the investigation into why, and turned it into a new direction for the product.

Surface

Role

Team

Problem

Seen by everyone, used by few

Nearly every learner saw Companion, and two thirds clicked on it. But only about one in four ever sent a message, and weekly usage kept falling.

Solution

Reframing the problem

Through analytics, session recordings and a survey of 173 learners, I showed the issue wasn't visibility or usability, but value: learners didn't see why they should use Companion rather than ChatGPT.

Context

Companion was designed to know each project's brief and expectations, something a general-purpose chatbot can't offer.

OpenClassrooms trains adults through online, career-focused degree programs built around real projects, with a human mentor for regular one-on-one sessions.

After launching on all paths on July 8, 2024, early data showed a clear lack of usage.

Goals

Understand why learners weren't using Companion.

  1. Separate the easy explanations (they can't find it, it's hard to use) from the harder ones (they don't need it).
  2. Give the squad evidence it could act on.

Process

I combined four sources over the first five weeks after launch.

Amplitude & Tableau

Tracked the funnel from project page to first message, and weekly retention.

Hotjar recordings

Checked for access and usability problems.

Survey, 173 answers

A survey I designed on usage, satisfaction, reasons for not using it, and comparison with other AI tools.

I also flagged the limits of the data, since the launch fell during the summer holidays.

Key insights

Insight 01

It wasn't a visibility or usability problem.

85.6% of active learners clicked on Companion, and most rated it easy to use. The real drop happened between opening it and asking a first question.

Image · 4:3 Adoption funnel Funnel chart: saw Companion → clicked → sent a first message, with the drop-off highlighted.

Insight 02

Companion was competing with ChatGPT, and losing on trust.

90% of learners already knew another AI tool. Learners did understand Companion's one edge (21 of them named its knowledge of the project), but many still found ChatGPT more reliable.

Image · 4:3 Survey results Chart of survey answers comparing Companion with other AI tools, or two or three learner verbatims.

Insight 03

It never became part of the learning routine.

Learners saw Companion around 36 times on average but used it on only 5% of those occasions. More than half stopped by their third week.

Image · 4:3 Weekly retention Retention curve over the first five weeks, showing the drop by week three.

Insight 04

Showing works better than telling.

Learners who attended a webinar demonstrating Companion were more likely to try it and to keep using it. A strong value proposition wasn't enough on its own; learners needed concrete use cases.

Image · 4:3 Webinar effect Comparison of usage between webinar attendees and other learners, or a screenshot of the webinar demo.

Recommendations

From these insights, I proposed five directions.

  1. Show concrete use cases in the chat and dashboard.
  2. Fix bugs and give Companion richer project context.
  3. Re-introduce it at each new project rather than only the first.
  4. Measure answer accuracy.
  5. Make Companion proactive on the problems learners most often face in a given project.

Reflections

Awareness isn't adoption.

A feature everyone sees can still fail; the real question is the first meaningful interaction.

An AI feature competes with the best AI the user already has.

The bar isn't "useful", it's "more useful than ChatGPT", and the only durable edge is context the general tool can't access.

Start from the need, not the capability.
